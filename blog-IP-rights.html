Intellectual Property Rights in the Creative Industry:

IP rights and their importance in the creative industries

Introduction

For many new creators, IP can be a complex or overly legal concept. Yet, it provides the framework that sustains creative work—protecting our art, code, stories, and designs from unauthorised use. In the game industry, where collaboration and innovation are ongoing, understanding IP isn't optional; it's vital.

Written By: Callum McGaffin - Games Development Student
Connect: LinkedIn, GitHub, X

So what exactly is Intellectual Property, and how does it matter in a field that thrives on innovation?

Intellectual Property in simple terms, refers to the legal rights that protect creations of the mind, such as inventions, designs, symbols, images, literary and artistic works used in commerce. Like other forms of property, it can be owned, bought, sold, licensed, or stolen. The key difference being that IP is intangible - it is created from ideas.

In the creative industries, ideas are currency. Whether you're producing music, film, fashion, or video games, your value often depends on the originality of what you create. Intellectual Property (IP) rights matter because they transform ideas into assets that can be owned, protected, and monetised — allowing creators to build sustainable careers rather than see their work copied or exploited.

There are many types of Intellectual Property, but for the Creative Industry there are five main forms:

- Copyright protects creative expressions like code, artwork, and music.
- Trademarks safeguard names, logos, and branding.
- Patents cover inventions or technical innovations.
- Design Rights protect the visual appearance of products.
- Trade Secrets protect confidential information, such as proprietary tools or workflows.

At their core, IP rights ensure that creators receive recognition and reward for their work.

Why IP Rights Matter in the Creative Industries

IP isn't just about defence — it's a system that drives growth. It motivates developers, studios, and artists to take creative risks, understanding their work can't simply be stolen or replicated without consequence.
When protected ideas can be safely shared, they can also be licensed, sold, or adapted, creating a cycle of collaboration and profit that sustains the entire industry.

Prominent examples in the gaming sphere are Minecraft and Five Nights at Freddy's. These creative works have grown into enormous properties, expanding from games into books, merchandise, collaborations, and even movies. Their values don't stem solely from gameplay, but also from the Intellectual Property rights that establish ownership of the characters, universe, and creative concepts behind them.

For developers, IP safeguards not only their work but also their reputation. It offers clarity in collaborations and helps prevent disputes over ownership — something that can make or break a project.

Intellectual property (IP) is not without controversy. Some argue that large corporations sometimes use IP law as a barrier to competition — building “walls” around ideas that might not be genuinely original.
For emerging developers, this tension is real. IP can feel empowering when it protects their own work — but intimidating when it's employed to fence off entire categories of creative expression.

Recent examples in the industry highlight how these issues play out on a larger scale:

Nintendo vs. Palworld (2024-25)
After Palworld's explosive success, Nintendo began filing patents around creature-catching mechanics, sparking debate on whether common gameplay ideas should be patentable. Critics argue that such claims risk limiting creative expression across the entire genre.
Warner Bros. and the “Nemesis System” Patent (2021)
By patenting its AI-driven enemy hierarchy system from Shadow of Mordor, Warner Bros. faced backlash for potentially stifling other developers from experimenting with dynamic storytelling mechanics.
Disney and AI Training Data (2025)
Disney has taken legal action against AI companies using its characters and storylines to train generative models. This raises important questions about creativity in the age of AI — who owns the data, and can machines “learn” from copyrighted art without permission?

Each case highlights the fine line between protection and restriction. For emerging creators, these examples are reminders that IP can empower innovation (or fence it off) depending on how it's used.

Do IP Rights Stifle Creativity?

While IP protection is essential, there's growing debate about whether it sometimes limits creativity — especially when it's used too broadly.

In game development, creativity is often iterative — new ideas build on existing ones. Every platformer owes something to Mario, and every open world owes something to Zelda or GTA. The challenge is to innovate within inspiration — to transform rather than copy.

When companies patent mechanics or claim ownership over common ideas, innovation can become restricted to those with legal power. The Nintendo and Warner Bros. examples show how large studios can dominate creative space, leaving smaller teams hesitant to experiment in fear of infringement.

IP should ideally protect the expression of a concept, not the concept itself. When applied this way, it doesn't stifle creativity, it protects the uniqueness of execution. For emerging developers, the key is balance: learning to respect IP boundaries while still experimenting boldly within them.

Finding Balance as a Creator

As someone new to the game industry, I really appreciate the delicate balance creators need to strike. On one side, IP provides a way to safeguard our work from misuse or exploitation, which is especially vital for small studios and freelancers who depend on their originality to shine. On the other side, it's noticeable how big corporations might leverage these laws to overshadow others or patent mechanics that could spark new ideas.

Recent disputes highlight how blurred the line has become between inspiration and infringement. These examples serve as a reminder that IP should never be about claiming ownership of ideas themselves, but rather about protecting the expression of those ideas.

The future of creativity — particularly in interactive and digital media — depends on maintaining this balance. IP must continue to safeguard passion and originality while remaining adaptable to new technologies such as AI.

I believe IP shouldn't hinder creativity — it should promote it. It should give creators the confidence to share, collaborate, and experiment, without fear that their contributions will be erased or exploited. For young developers like me, understanding IP isn't just about legal protection; it's about creative empowerment. It's about recognising the value of what I make, while still appreciating the importance of openness and collaboration that drive the entire creative world forward.
